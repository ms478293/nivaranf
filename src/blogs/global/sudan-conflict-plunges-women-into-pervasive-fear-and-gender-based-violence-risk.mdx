The protracted conflict in Sudan has plunged the nation into a profound humanitarian crisis, with women and girls bearing an exceptionally heavy and often hidden burden. A stark assessment from the United Nations reveals that a staggering three-quarters of Sudanese women now live with a pervasive sense of insecurity, feeling unsafe as the war continues to rage across their communities. This widespread fear is not merely anecdotal; it reflects a grim reality of escalating dangers, particularly the heightened risk of gender-based violence (GBV), a threat that shadows their every move, whether they are desperately seeking refuge or attempting to rebuild their lives in displacement camps. The UN's reproductive and sexual health agency, UNFPA, has consistently highlighted the dire circumstances, underscoring a consistent narrative from women and girls across the war-torn nation regarding their continuous exposure to peril and the looming specter of violence.

The very fabric of safety has unravelled in Sudan since the conflict erupted, transforming once-familiar streets and homes into zones of unpredictable danger. Women navigating this landscape face direct threats from active hostilities, including indiscriminate shelling, gunfire, and the breakdown of law and order that often accompanies armed conflict. Beyond the immediate violence of combat, the absence of effective governance and the proliferation of armed groups create an environment ripe for opportunistic crime and targeted abuses. Essential public services have collapsed in many areas, leaving women without access to police protection, legal recourse, or even basic medical attention, further isolating them and amplifying their vulnerability to various forms of violence and exploitation.

The act of fleeing itself, a desperate measure to escape the immediate conflict, often exposes women and girls to a new set of grave risks. Journeys to perceived safety are fraught with peril, involving long treks through insecure territories, encounters with armed elements, and the constant threat of abduction, sexual assault, or forced recruitment. Checkpoints, often manned by various factions, can become choke points for exploitation. Upon arrival at displacement camps or informal settlements, the dangers do not cease. Overcrowded conditions, inadequate lighting, insufficient sanitation facilities, and a severe lack of privacy create environments where GBV thrives. Women and girls often face the impossible choice between venturing out for essential resources, like water or firewood, and risking assault, or enduring deprivation within the perceived safety of their shelters.

Gender-based violence in Sudan manifests in a horrifying spectrum, ranging from sexual violence, including rape and sexual slavery, to forced marriage, exploitation, and exacerbated domestic violence. The stress and trauma of conflict, coupled with economic hardship and the breakdown of social norms, often lead to an increase in intimate partner violence within households. For adolescent girls, the threat of early or forced marriage is a grim reality, sometimes seen by desperate families as a means of protection or survival. The pervasive fear reported by women is deeply rooted in these tangible threats, creating a climate of terror that impacts their mental and physical well-being long after the immediate danger has passed. The true scale of GBV remains largely hidden due to stigma, fear of reprisal, and the collapse of reporting mechanisms.

The health consequences of this pervasive insecurity and GBV are catastrophic and far-reaching. Beyond the immediate physical injuries sustained from violence, women and girls endure profound psychological trauma, including severe anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder, often without access to mental health support. Reproductive health services, already fragile before the conflict, have been decimated, leaving pregnant women without antenatal care, safe delivery options, or emergency obstetric services. The lack of access to contraception increases unintended pregnancies in dangerous circumstances, while the absence of post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) and treatment for sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV, following sexual violence, compounds the crisis. The denial of these fundamental health rights represents a critical failure in humanitarian response.

Humanitarian organizations, including UNFPA, are striving to address these complex needs amidst immense operational challenges. Access to affected populations is frequently hampered by active fighting, bureaucratic impediments, and the sheer scale of displacement. Funding shortfalls remain a persistent obstacle, limiting the reach and scope of vital protection and health programs. Aid workers themselves operate under extreme duress, facing security risks and logistical nightmares. Despite these hurdles, efforts are underway to establish safe spaces, provide psychosocial support, distribute dignity kits, and offer basic reproductive health services. However, these interventions, while critical, are often a drop in the ocean compared to the overwhelming and escalating needs of millions.

The enduring impact of this conflict and the pervasive threat of GBV will scar Sudanese society for generations. The trauma inflicted upon women and girls undermines social cohesion, erodes trust within communities, and hinders future development. Children born of sexual violence face immense stigma and challenges. The loss of education opportunities for girls, forced into early marriage or labor, will have long-term repercussions on human capital and economic recovery. Rebuilding a society where half the population lives in constant fear and experiences such profound violations of their rights will require sustained, comprehensive efforts extending far beyond the cessation of hostilities, focusing on justice, reconciliation, and robust social support systems.

The international community bears a significant responsibility to act decisively. This includes not only increasing humanitarian funding and ensuring unhindered access for aid organizations but also exerting diplomatic pressure to end the conflict and hold perpetrators of GBV accountable. Robust protection mechanisms must be prioritized in all humanitarian responses, with specific attention to the unique vulnerabilities of women and girls. Furthermore, long-term investments in strengthening local women's organizations and community-led initiatives are crucial, as these groups are often best placed to understand and address the specific needs and cultural contexts of their communities.

The crisis in Sudan is a stark reminder of how conflict disproportionately impacts the most vulnerable, particularly women and girls whose fundamental rights to safety, health, and dignity are systematically eroded. The chilling statistic that three-quarters of Sudanese women feel unsafe is not just a number; it represents millions of individual stories of fear, trauma, and resilience in the face of unimaginable adversity. As the war continues, the global imperative to protect these lives, provide essential support, and advocate tirelessly for a lasting peace becomes ever more urgent. The world must not turn a blind eye to the silent suffering unfolding in Sudan, demanding an immediate end to the violence and a concerted effort to restore safety and hope for its women and girls.
